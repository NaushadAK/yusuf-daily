Top story

Iran says three tankers hit mines in Hormuz despite US escort

Iran's Revolutionary Guards said three oil tankers struck naval mines and caught fire in the Strait of Hormuz late on Monday, even though the ships were sailing with US military support. Britain's maritime trade office said there were no casualties, and the claims have not been independently confirmed. On the same day the US Treasury widened its sanctions on Iran, targeting more than two dozen exchange houses, front companies and officials. The UN's shipping agency has counted 136 security incidents involving ships in the region since March, which keeps pressure on oil supplies to countries like India.

Something to think about

Why a tanker can survive a hit that would sink a smaller ship

Today's top story has tankers striking mines and catching fire, yet staying afloat. One reason is the double hull: modern oil tankers have two layers of steel with a gap of about two metres between them, so a hole in the outer skin does not immediately let oil out or sea water in. Double hulls became compulsory for new tankers in the 1990s, after the 1989 Exxon Valdez spill in Alaska showed how much damage a single torn hull could cause.

Tankers are also divided into many separate cargo tanks and bulkheads, so flooding in one section does not spread through the whole ship. Designers trade off extra steel, weight and cost against safety, which is a classic mechanical and naval engineering problem.

Question for the day: If you were designing a ship's hull, how would you decide how wide the gap between the two layers should be, and what would you give up by making it wider?
